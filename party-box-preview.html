Why the deck changed

Built for the biggest reaction

A blank beats a question A question invites an explanation, and explanations are wordy. A blank forces a single punchy noun — which is where the laugh actually lives.

The real reason the WiFi is down

The vending machine has started dispensing

Vivid setup, wide slot The scene should be specific enough to picture instantly, but the blank should accept almost anything. Narrow scenes with wide slots produce the most range between players.
Funny before the answer If a prompt doesn't get a reaction the second it hits the TV, it won't rescue a weak answer either. Every prompt has to earn its own laugh first.
No obvious right answer If everyone writes the same thing, there's no contest and no vote worth casting. Prompts with one dominant answer got cut.

The middle ground

Cards vs. a blank box

Both approaches break differently Cards Against Humanity hands you cards, so nobody freezes — but you can only play what you were dealt. A blank box has no ceiling, and it also strands anyone who isn't a natural writer. In a party that means two people take every vote and everyone else quietly checks out.
So: seeds, not answers Five short scraps under your text box. Tap one and it drops in at the cursor. Tap two and they stack. Or write your own and never look at them. The answer stays yours, which keeps the ceiling where it was.

Prompts about the room

Your own stories, weaponised

Everyone answers one question first In the lobby, each phone shows a sentence to finish — "the time I…", "I once got in trouble for…". Ten seconds, and it's optional.
Two rounds use those answers Dropped at random points, so they arrive as a surprise rather than always landing on the same beat.

You only ever supply a story about yourself Never about someone else. Player-writes-about-player turns cruel in about two rounds and doesn't need a game to do it. Self-supplied material stays affectionate — the target handed it over.
Opt out any time Anyone can decline to be named and still play everything else, and the host can switch the whole thing off from the lobby.

How a round works

Three rounds, same shape

  1. Everyone gets the promptIt shows on the TV. Same one for the whole room.no clock
  2. Fill in the blankOn your phone, in private, with five seeds to borrow if you're stuck. 90 characters, so keep it punchy.60 seconds
  3. Answers go up anonymouslyThe TV shows every answer with no names attached.—
  4. Vote for your favoriteTap the one that got you. You can't vote for yourself.25 seconds
  5. The revealNames come out, votes get counted, then it rolls into the next round.12 seconds
It runs itself. Every phase is on a timer, so nobody touches the laptop mid-game. If everyone answers early it jumps ahead instead of waiting — and if someone wanders off, the clock moves past them.

Scoring

One rule, basically

100 points per voteEvery person who picks your answer is 100 points to you.
Votes are the only way to score. No points for voting, no bonus for speed.
You can't vote for yourself — the game hides your own answer from your list.
Miss the clock and you write nothing that round. No answer, no votes, no points.
Highest total after 3 rounds wins. Ties just tie — no tiebreaker.
Round 1 — 2 people picked you200
Round 2 — nobody picked you0
Round 3 — 3 people picked you300
Final500
What this rewards. Writing for the room, not for yourself. The answer that makes five people laugh beats the one that makes you laugh hardest.
